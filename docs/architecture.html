VR SimReady Physics: how the parts interact

A Meta Quest 3 app over Link that renders a USD scene with NVIDIA's ovxr / ovrtx stack, simulates it with ovphysx (PhysX), and lets you add SimReady assets from Hugging Face by voice or from an in‑VR catalog, then grab, throw and fix them in place.

C++ app: everything per frame Python sidecar: everything slow External service or device

System overview

Two processes share the work. The native app cpp/build/vrsim_cpp.exe owns the OpenXR session, the shared USD stage, the renderer and physics, and must hit the 72 Hz frame budget. The Python sidecar vrsim/sidecar.py does work that may take seconds: speech recognition, catalog search, downloads, USD preparation, and rasterizing HUD text and catalog pages. They talk over one localhost TCP socket carrying JSON lines. Large data never crosses the socket; the sidecar writes USD layers to .cache/generated/ and sends their paths.

Components

Quest 3 over Link device

The Meta OpenXR runtime on the PC renders to the headset over Quest Link. ovxr creates the OpenXR session; XrInput borrows its handles and adds its own action set for the Touch controllers. The headset microphone shows up as the Windows input device “Headset Microphone (Oculus Virtual Audio Device)”, which the sidecar records directly.

ovxr + ovrtx renderer C++

ovxr runs the split frame loop (begin_frame → bind_frame → submit_frame) and renders the stage with ovrtx. Two modes, chosen with --render:

  • RT2: real-time path tracing with the HDRI dome sky. Bind costs about 9–11 ms per frame; the headset runs between 36 and 72 fps.
  • Minimal: rasterized, with a generated backdrop sky. Holds 72 fps.

ovstage shared stage C++

One USD stage seen by both renderer and physics. The app holds one ordinal at a time: per-frame transform writes go into ordinal N, which bind_frame seals. Population changes (spawn, remove, catalog page) are committed at their own sealed ordinals. ovphysx drains only those ordinals, never the per-frame transforms it produced itself.

ovphysx (PhysX) C++

physics.cpp attaches ovphysx to the stage, steps at a fixed 1/72 s with 144 Hz substeps (at most 3 steps per frame), reads every dynamic body's pose in one query, and writes linear/angular velocities. Velocity writes use cached selection queries per prop and body set, released when a prop is removed.

Python sidecar Python

Started with uv run python -m vrsim.sidecar. Writes the environment layers at startup, then waits on port 47800. Worker pools keep slow jobs off the socket thread: spawn/download, catalog page building, and hover info. A Feedback board turns any status message into HUD geometry.

Wit.ai voice service

Holding A or X records audio (with 0.5 s pre-roll); on release it goes to Wit.ai's /speech endpoint. Trained intents are used when confident, otherwise regexes parse the transcript: add a mug, show me balls, next page, undo, clear all, fix it, free it, cancel.

SimReady on Hugging Face service

The catalog merges the nvidia/simready-catalog index with a crawl of partner repos (about 2,800 assets, cached in .cache/catalog_index.json). Searches go to SimReady Central (NVIDIA USD Search on an HF Space) first, which also returns bounding-box dimensions, and fall back to the local index. Packages download with the Hub client into the HF cache.

Generated USD layers Python

scene.py writes the environment (Studierstube scene, floor and colliders, lights, hands, HUD panel, decals) in RT2 and Minimal variants. assets.py writes one wrapper per asset that normalizes it to Z-up metres. browser.py writes each catalog page as a layer of textured quads.

One frame, step by step

From main.cpp's loop. Times are averages from the app's status log on an RTX 6000 Ada.

  1. ovxr_begin_frame

    Gets the frame plan and predicted display time. If the OpenXR session isn't running (headset off), the loop only drains sidecar messages and sleeps 50 ms.

  2. XrInput::sync

    Controller grip and aim poses at the predicted display time, plus button edges. Then buttons (talk, undo, clear), catalog grab, hand interaction (grab, release, trigger-to-fix), catalog pointer and the HUD close button.

  3. driveGrabs · driveAnchors

    Every held or fixed body gets one velocity that reaches its target pose by the end of this frame's physics steps: v = (target − pose) / (steps · dt), clamped to 12 m/s and 30 rad/s. About 0.0–0.1 ms.

  4. Physics::step × 1–3

    Fixed 1/72 s steps against real time. 0.25–0.5 ms per frame.

  5. readBodies · writeBodies · writeVisuals

    Body poses out of ovphysx, then body, hand, ray, HUD, catalog and spinner transforms (and changed HUD text points) written to the stage at ordinal N. About 0.2 ms.

  6. ovxr_bind_frame(N)

    Seals ordinal N and renders it. This is where the frame time goes: about 9–11 ms in RT2.

  7. ovxr_submit_frame · handleSidecar

    Submits to the compositor (about 1 ms), then applies queued sidecar messages: spawns, removals, catalog pages, HUD text.

Sidecar protocol

JSON objects, one per line, over 127.0.0.1:47800. The sidecar listens; the app connects (and reconnects) from a background thread and drains incoming messages between frames. On every connect the sidecar resends the welcome HUD text and spawns the desk props.

Adding an asset

  1. voice or catalog

    “Add a ratchet” is parsed into a spawn command; picking a catalog tile sends browser_pick. “Show me balls” opens the catalog instead: the sidecar searches, downloads 256 px thumbnails and writes a page layer.

  2. catalog.py · search + fetch

    SimReady Central or the local index picks the package; the package files (no thumbnails or metadata) download with progress on the HUD, cancellable by voice or ✕.

  3. assets.describe_asset

    Writes the wrapper layer: an up-axis and metres-per-unit correction, and overs that turn instancing off (ovphysx skips colliders inside instances). Reads the rigid bodies, a bounding sphere per body, which bodies are jointed parts (body1 of a non-fixed joint), the bottom height and the size.

  4. spawn message → Stage::addReference

    The app builds a tiny layer /World/Props/prop_NNNN with the spawn pose times the correction, referencing the wrapper's Asset prim, and commits it at its own ordinal.

  5. Physics::sync(ordinal)

    ovphysx drains that ordinal and creates the bodies, colliders and joints. The app interns the body paths and maps each to (prop, body) for picking.

  6. placement

    Before anything spawns, a red marker follows the ray of one hand onto upward-facing scene surfaces (normal z > 0.6). Its arrow shows the asset's facing and the thumbstick turns it. The trigger spawns the asset on that spot, standing on it via its bottom_z, and fixes it there as soon as physics reports its bodies. B or “undo” cancels. A batch stands in a row across the ray. Desk props (the NVIDIA mug) spawn exactly where they stood in the scene. At most 40 props; the oldest is removed first.

surfaces The sidecar exports the environment colliders' convex hulls (.cache/generated/placement_hulls.bin: per hull, bounds and planes). The app casts the pointer ray against them with a slab test and then plane clipping, so it hits the same shapes PhysX collides with. Thumbstick while holding a fixed prop turns its anchors about the centre's vertical axis, keeping their height, so it stays standing on the floor.

Interaction model

Controls

  • Squeeze: grab what is within 12 cm of the hand; release to throw (hand velocity × 1.3).
  • Squeeze while pointing: force-pull the pointed-at prop to the hand.
  • Trigger: pointer ray for props and catalog tiles; while holding a prop, fix it or free it.
  • A / X: push-to-talk. B: undo last spawn. Y: clear all.
  • Thumbstick click: open or close the catalog. Flick: page it.

Velocity-driven holding

Held bodies are never teleported. Each frame they get the velocity that reaches the hand target by the end of the frame's physics, so contacts stay physical (a held mug pushes others and is stopped by the desk), and the last velocity becomes the throw. One velocity per frame for all substeps; re-applying a one-step velocity before every step overshot and made held props jitter.

Jointed assets

The first hand on a prop holds all its bodies rigidly, keeping their poses relative to the touched body. While it does, the other hand can take a movable part (a lid, a glasses arm) and drive it alone; the joint keeps it attached. Grabbing a non-movable part transfers the whole prop. Releasing either hand recaptures relative poses, with no throw.

Fix in place

Trigger while holding a prop, or “fix it”, stores each body's pose as an anchor. Every frame the non-movable bodies are driven back to their anchors with the same velocity drive, so they stay put (a hit nudges them about 1 mm and they return). Grabbing the base does nothing; jointed parts can be pulled with either hand. Trigger while holding it again, or “free it”, releases it.

HUD, catalog and scene details

HUD panel

Real scene geometry at /World/HUD/Panel: a backing, a text mesh with a fixed set of quads, and coloured status bars. Pillow rasterizes the text into merged pixel rectangles; a message change is one points write at the frame's ordinal. The panel lazily follows the head, 35° to the left, clear of the catalog.

Catalog hover info

When the pointer settles on a tile for 0.25 s, the sidecar posts its size and download size, e.g. “11.4 × 9.0 × 8.0 cm · 4.2 MB download (30 files) · already downloaded”. Dimensions come from SimReady Central or are measured from a downloaded root USD.

Desk props and decals

Scene objects listed in config.desk_props (the NVIDIA mug) are hidden in the environment and re-spawned as rigid bodies at their original position. Logo decals (OpenUSD on the laptop screen, NVIDIA wrapped around the mug) are alpha-cutout textured meshes authored by scene.py.

The Newton edition

vrsim/app.py is the earlier all-Python app: the same ovxr loop, controllers, voice and catalog, but with NVIDIA Newton for physics and the HUD in-process. The C++ edition and its sidecar replaced it for speed.

Start-up

start_vr.bat (Minimal) or start_vr_rt2.bat opens the sidecar in its own window, waits until port 47800 is listening (without connecting, since the sidecar takes the first connection as the app), then runs vrsim_cpp.exe --render minimal|rt2. The app waits until the headset session starts. Tokens for Wit.ai and Hugging Face are read from local files that git ignores.

Known limitations

LimitationWhy
Articulated assets (e.g. the compressor) can't be grabbed per partThe app's rigid-body queries skip articulation links; their poses would need ARTICULATION_LINK reads.
No scene raycastsovphysx exposes no raycast or sweep API, so picking uses per-body bounding spheres and placement rays are cast against hull planes the sidecar exports. Props are not hulls, so nothing can be placed on top of another prop.
RT2 frame ratePath tracing at Quest resolution costs about 10 ms of bind plus GPU time; the runtime falls back to 36 fps.
Fixed props are held by velocity, not made kinematicSwitching a body to kinematic at runtime isn't exposed; the velocity anchor is stiff but can be nudged slightly.
Minimal mode sky and shadowsThe backdrop sky is generated; the RT2 dome sun doesn't line up exactly with the configured sun azimuth.
